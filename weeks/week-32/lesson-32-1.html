📖 Today's Focus
How did rivers and seaports decide the western half of the war?

While the Army of the Potomac struggled in Virginia, a different war was going on in the West and along the coast. It was fought on rivers, in swamps, and from the decks of ships. Two commanders, Ulysses S. Grant and David Farragut, won victories there that Lincoln badly needed. This lesson follows them.

Rivers as Roads

In the 1860s a river was like a highway. The Mississippi, the Tennessee, and the Cumberland carried troops, food, and cotton. Whoever controlled them could move armies deep into enemy land. The Union’s plan was to take the rivers, split the Confederacy, and shut off its ports. Remember General Scott’s Anaconda Plan. In 1862 it began to work.

Forts Henry and Donelson

In February 1862 a little-known general named Ulysses S. Grant moved against two Confederate forts in Tennessee. Fort Henry guarded the Tennessee River and Fort Donelson guarded the Cumberland. Union gunboats, built in a few months by James Eads of St. Louis, who covered river steamers with iron plates, joined the attack. Under Admiral Andrew Foote they forced Fort Henry to give up on February 6. Ten days later Grant surrounded Fort Donelson. The Confederate commander asked for terms. Grant replied that he would accept nothing except an unconditional surrender. Some 12,000 Confederate soldiers gave up. The North cheered and the newspapers said his initials meant “Unconditional Surrender” Grant.

The victory opened Tennessee. Nashville fell, and Kentucky was safe. Before the war Grant had left the army and failed at farming and business, and he was working in his family’s leather store in Illinois when the fighting began. He had a quiet manner and a plain uniform, but he was stubborn and he did not give up. Those traits were what the Union army most needed.

Shiloh

In April Grant’s army camped at a place called Pittsburg Landing, near a small church named Shiloh. Early on April 6, the Confederates under Albert Sidney Johnston and P.G.T. Beauregard attacked without warning. For a day the Union army was driven back. Johnston, one of the Confederacy’s most respected generals, was hit by a bullet and bled to death. Had he lived, the battle might have gone differently. The next morning reinforcements arrived, and Grant counterattacked and won.

Shiloh shocked the country. In two days, about 23,000 men were killed, wounded, or missing. That was more than in all the previous American wars put together. Grant came away believing the war would not be won by half measures. When critics demanded that Lincoln remove him, Lincoln answered, “I can’t spare this man. He fights.”

Farragut Takes New Orleans

New Orleans was the largest city in the South and the mouth of the Mississippi River. It was defended by two forts and a chain of obstacles in the river. In April 1862, Admiral David Farragut led a fleet up the river. He ran past the forts at night, with cannon fire lighting the sky. The city had no chance and surrendered on April 25. Cotton and sugar could no longer leave by the Mississippi. The Confederacy had lost its greatest port.

Ironclads and the Blockade

The sea mattered too. In March 1862 the Confederates covered an old ship with iron plates and named it the Virginia. It destroyed two wooden Union warships in a single day. The next day, a Union ironclad called the Monitor, which looked like a raft with a round tower on top, arrived to fight it. The two ships battled to a draw. But the age of wooden warships was over.

The Union navy was also working on its blockade. The Southern coast was thousands of miles long, with many small harbors, so at first many “blockade runners” slipped through. But the Navy grew from about ninety ships to hundreds, and the net tightened. Southern cotton piled up on docks. Medicine, shoes, and machines became hard to buy, and prices rose.

Florida and the Coast

Florida had a long coastline, and it was part of this story. The Union Navy held Key West and Fort Pickens, and used them as bases. Union forces took Fernandina, St. Augustine, and Jacksonville in 1862, though they sometimes withdrew. The Confederates relied on Florida for cattle, which fed their armies, and for salt, which was used to preserve food. Union raiders destroyed many salt works along the coast. Small boats slipped in and out of Florida’s bays carrying cotton and supplies.

What Was Left

Texas and the land west of the Mississippi were called the Trans-Mississippi. Cut off from the East, Texas found a way around the blockade. Its cotton moved by wagon to the Mexican port of Matamoros, where ships carried it to Europe. By the end of 1862 the Union held the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers, the mouth of the Mississippi, and much of the Southern coast. But one great obstacle remained. The Confederate fortress of Vicksburg, high on a bluff, still blocked the river. Lincoln said that Vicksburg was the key to the war.
